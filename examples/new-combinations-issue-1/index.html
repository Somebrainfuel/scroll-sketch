Big yeses, made small

Somewhere now, a small brand owner is looking at a minimum production order of 5,000 units and wondering whether they'll sell. Another is working out whether they can afford to hire someone for marketing.

Big yeses are where good ideas stagnate. Here are three businesses trying to make those decisions smaller. Scroll slowly: the charts draw themselves as you go.

The curve is a sketch of the idea, not measured data.

These three don't fit one neat theory. 120ft and Holo lower costs and make capabilities more accessible. IKEA is finding a way into transactions that were already happening without it. What they share is combination: buyers with sellers, brands with a shared factory, AI with a firm's own brand.

The late Harvard professor Clayton Christensen argued that some of the largest new markets come from people who weren't buying at all. When a decision feels big, customers rarely say no. They just don't decide. Your biggest competitor may be "not yet".

Small business · AI

Holo: a marketing team for about $21 a month

Vilnius startup Holo has raised €5 million for an AI service that plans and produces marketing for small businesses: ads, social posts, emails and website content. Its growth is the striking part.

Figures as reported by The Recursive. The $21 is my rough calculation.

Think of that as a fully automated marketing production team that can move fast. It gives a small business enormous marketing capability at a fraction of the cost of an agency.

There's a catch. In one experiment, writers given ideas by AI produced stories rated as more creative, but about 11% more similar to one another. If thousands of firms use similar AI marketing tools, what makes one stand out? Knowing what's worth saying, rather than producing more.

Big business · circular

IKEA: joining a market that already existed

IKEA has launched its marketplace for used IKEA furniture in the UK and Ireland. Follow one piece of furniture.

My guess is that the bigger prize is information: which products hold their value, and which simply get passed on. With AI to analyse it, IKEA can see which items are resold most and change the design.

If what you sell gets resold, refurbished or repaired, a market already exists around it. Someone will run your product's second life. Could it be you?
